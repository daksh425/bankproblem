A score above 750 means you have not defaulted. That is genuinely all it means.
It gets your file past the automated filter and onto a credit officer's screen,
where a completely different set of questions gets asked.

Here is what rejects a 780 applicant.

## 1. Your FOIR is too high

FOIR — fixed obligation to income ratio — is the share of your monthly income
already committed to EMIs and other fixed payments. Most lenders want the
proposed EMI plus your existing obligations to stay under roughly 50 to 55% of
net monthly income, lower for smaller incomes.

Your score says nothing about this. You can have a flawless repayment record and
still be rejected because you are already servicing a car loan, two personal
loans and a credit card minimum. From the lender's side, perfect history is
irrelevant if the arithmetic says the next EMI does not fit.

<Callout type="warn" title="The cheapest fix is usually a card balance">
Revolving card balances count towards your obligations at an assumed minimum
payment. Clearing a ₹80,000 card balance can move your FOIR more than a salary
rise would, and it takes one payment rather than a job change.
</Callout>

## 2. Thin file, or the wrong kind of history

A 780 built entirely on one credit card used lightly for three years is a thin
file. The lender has no evidence of how you behave with a large, long-dated,
fixed obligation, because you have never had one.

This is why high scorers in their twenties get rejected for large personal loans
while someone with a 730 and a cleanly repaid car loan sails through. The second
file answers the question being asked. The first does not.

## 3. Too many recent enquiries

Every application creates a hard enquiry. Several inside a few weeks reads as
someone shopping desperately or stacking credit, and most lenders filter on it
regardless of score.

The trap is comparison sites: "check your eligibility across 12 lenders" can
generate real enquiries at several of them. Check your own report directly
instead — that is a soft enquiry and affects nothing.

## 4. Employer, income documentation, or location

Lenders maintain internal lists: employer categories, negative-list companies,
pincodes where collection is hard, minimum vintage in the current job. Being
rejected for any of these has nothing to do with you personally and nothing to
do with your score.

Self-employed applicants hit this hardest. Income that is real but shows up
irregularly across bank statements is treated as weaker than a smaller, perfectly
regular salary credit.

## 5. Something on your report that is not yours

This is the one worth checking before you do anything else, because it is both
common and fixable.

A guarantor entry you forgot about, a settled account that belongs to someone
with a similar name, an old loan still showing as open after you closed it, or a
written-off amount nobody told you about. All of these can coexist with a good
score and still cause a rejection, because the officer is reading the report, not
just the number at the top.

<Callout type="rule" title="You are owed ₹100 a day if it is not fixed in 30 days" cite="RBI (Credit Information Reporting) Directions, 2025" citeUrl="https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/BS_ViewMasDirections.aspx?id=12764">
Credit bureaus and lenders must resolve a credit information complaint within
**30 days**. Beyond that you are entitled to **₹100 for every day of delay**,
shared between the bureau and whichever party caused it.
</Callout>

## What to actually do next

Pull all four reports before you reapply. Lenders do not all pull from the same
bureau, so an error that exists on one and not the others is a plausible
explanation for an inconsistent set of outcomes.
[The four reports often disagree for entirely ordinary reasons](/credit-score/why-credit-scores-differ-across-bureaus),
so knowing which bureau your lender uses matters.

Then work out your own FOIR honestly, clear what you can, and wait. Do not
reapply in the same month at four more lenders; that converts one rejection into
a pattern.

If a lender refuses to give any reason in writing, and its own fair practices
code says it will, that is a grievance worth escalating.
[The RBI Ombudsman route is free](/disputes/rbi-ombudsman-how-to-file) — though
be clear-eyed that the Ombudsman will not second-guess a lender's commercial
judgement. It will take up a refusal to follow a published process.
