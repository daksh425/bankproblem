This is the first decision in NRI banking and the one most often got wrong,
usually because it is treated as a choice. It is not. The **source of the money**
decides the account.

## The distinction

<div class="tablewrap">

| | NRE account | NRO account |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What goes in | Foreign earnings remitted to India | Income arising in India |
| Typical credits | Salary sent from abroad, foreign savings | Rent, dividends, interest, pension, sale proceeds |
| Currency held in | Indian rupees | Indian rupees |
| Tax on interest in India | Exempt while you are non-resident | Taxable, with TDS at the non-resident rate |
| Repatriable | Freely, principal and interest | Up to USD 1 million per financial year, with documentation |
| Joint holding | With another NRI, and with a resident relative on former-or-survivor basis | With another NRI or with a resident |

</div>

<Callout type="rule" title="Source decides the account, not convenience">
Money **earned abroad** and sent to India belongs in the NRE account. Money
**arising in India** belongs in the NRO account. You cannot credit Indian rent to
an NRE account to make it repatriable and tax-free, and doing so creates a problem
the bank will eventually find.
</Callout>

## Why the NRE account is attractive

Interest is exempt from Indian income tax while you are non-resident, and both
principal and interest are freely repatriable. There is no cap and no annual
certificate requirement to send the money back out.

That combination is why foreign earnings should go here rather than into an NRO
account.

The catch worth knowing: NRE balances are held in rupees. If the rupee weakens
against your home currency, you carry that loss on conversion back. The tax
exemption is not a free lunch.

## Why you still need the NRO account

Because Indian income has to go somewhere, and it is not permitted to go into an
NRE account. Rent from a flat, dividends, interest on old deposits, a pension,
proceeds from selling property — all NRO.

Repatriation from NRO is possible but capped and documented: **up to USD 1 million
per financial year**, with a chartered accountant's certification.
[How that process works](/nri/nro-repatriation-limit-one-million).

## The common mistakes

**Keeping the old resident account.** Not an option once you are non-resident; it
must be redesignated as NRO.
[What that involves](/nri/resident-account-after-moving-abroad).

**Routing rent into the NRE account** because it is simpler. It creates an
unexplained credit the bank must question, and it does not make the money
repatriable.

**Assuming NRE exemption means tax-free everywhere.** It is an exemption under
Indian law. Your country of residence may well tax the interest, and many
treaties require you to declare it.

**Forgetting the demat account and mutual fund folios.** They have their own
NRE/NRO-linked structures and the same source rules apply.

## Set it up properly once

Open both. Put foreign earnings in the NRE, Indian income in the NRO, and keep
the two streams clean. Everything downstream — repatriation, tax filing, a
property sale — is dramatically easier when the paper trail is unambiguous.
