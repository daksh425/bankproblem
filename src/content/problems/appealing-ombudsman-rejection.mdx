Most rejections come down to one of three things, and only one of them is worth
appealing.

## Work out which rejection you got

**Rejected as outside the scheme.** The complaint was about a commercial decision
— a loan refused on credit grounds, a product the bank chose not to offer you.
The Ombudsman does not review commercial judgement, and an appeal on these facts
will go the same way. The route here is a consumer commission, if there is a
service deficiency in how the decision was handled.

**Rejected as premature or time-barred.** You filed before 30 days had passed
since your written complaint, or more than a year after the bank's reply. If it
was premature, simply refile once the 30 days are up. If it was time-barred, the
Ombudsman door has closed.

**Rejected on the merits.** The Ombudsman looked at the facts and sided with the
bank. This is the one an appeal is for.

<Callout type="warn" title="Read the rejection letter for which one it is">
The three look similar at a glance and the remedy is completely different. A
premature rejection is good news — refile. An out-of-scope rejection means
appealing is wasted effort. Only a merits rejection justifies the appeal.
</Callout>

## The appeal

You have **30 days from receiving** the award or rejection to appeal to the
Appellate Authority at RBI. It is free and needs no lawyer.

An appeal is not a second attempt at the same letter. It should identify
specifically what the Ombudsman got wrong:

- A document you submitted that was not considered.
- A rule or circular that applies and was not addressed.
- A factual finding that is contradicted by evidence already on the file.

"I disagree with the outcome" is not a ground. "The decision records that I
reported on 12 March; the bank's own acknowledgement, already on file, is dated 4
March, and the entire liability calculation turns on that date" is.

Attach the original complaint, the award or rejection, and the specific documents
you are pointing at. Nothing new that you could have filed the first time.

## If the appeal also fails, or the route was never right

A **consumer commission** handles deficiency of service and can award compensation
and costs. It is slower, may involve a fee, and is worth it mainly for larger
amounts — but it is not limited the way the Ombudsman scheme is, and it can look
at matters the Ombudsman had to decline.

[How the two compare, and which to choose](/disputes/consumer-court-vs-ombudsman).

## Before you appeal, check the complaint itself

A surprising share of merits rejections trace back to a complaint that never
clearly stated the rule or the remedy. If your original filing described how badly
you were treated rather than which obligation the bank missed, the appeal is a
chance to fix that framing.
