Most people lose money here not because the rules are against them, but because
they spend the first two days arguing with a call centre instead of filing a
written report. The refund you are entitled to is decided almost entirely by one
number: how many working days passed between you noticing the debit and you
telling the bank.

## The three bands

RBI's 2017 circular splits unauthorised transactions into three cases.

<Callout type="rule" title="Zero liability — bank pays everything" cite="RBI circular DBR.No.Leg.BC.78/09.07.005/2017-18, para 6" citeUrl="https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=11040">
You owe nothing if the loss came from a **deficiency on the bank's side** —
whether or not you reported it quickly — or if it came from a **third party**,
the fault lay with neither you nor the bank, and you **told the bank within
three working days** of receiving its communication about the transaction.
</Callout>

If you report on working day four through seven, your liability is capped by
account type:

<div class="tablewrap">

| Account or instrument | Your maximum liability |
| --- | --- |
| BSBD (basic savings) accounts | ₹5,000 |
| Other savings accounts; prepaid instruments and gift cards; current, cash credit and overdraft accounts of MSMEs; current accounts of individuals with annual turnover up to ₹25 lakh; credit cards with limit up to ₹5 lakh | ₹10,000 |
| All other current, cash credit and overdraft accounts; credit cards with limit above ₹5 lakh | ₹25,000 |

</div>

Report after seven working days and the loss falls under your bank's own
board-approved policy. That policy is usually unfavourable, and this is where
most people end up out of pocket.

<Callout type="warn" title="The deadline is working days, not calendar days">
Weekends and bank holidays do not count against you. A debit you notice on a
Friday evening still leaves you inside the three-working-day window if you
report on the following Wednesday. Do not take the risk — but know the rule when
a bank tells you that you are out of time.
</Callout>

## Where negligence changes things

If the loss happened because you shared credentials — OTP, PIN, CVV, card
details, net banking password — you bear the entire loss **until you report it**.

The part banks rarely mention: the moment you notify them, their liability
begins. Any transaction after your report is on the bank, no matter how the
original compromise happened. If your card was skimmed on Monday and you
reported it on Wednesday, everything after Wednesday is theirs.

The burden of proof is also not yours. The circular puts the onus on the bank to
prove customer negligence. In practice banks assert it rather than prove it, and
asserting it is not the same thing.

## The 10-working-day credit

This is the provision most worth knowing, because almost nobody invokes it.

<Callout type="rule" title="Your money comes back before the investigation ends" cite="RBI circular DBR.No.Leg.BC.78/09.07.005/2017-18, para 9" citeUrl="https://www.rbi.org.in/Scripts/NotificationUser.aspx?Id=11040">
On being notified, the bank must credit the disputed amount to your account
**within 10 working days from the date of your notification**. It does not get to
hold your money while it investigates. The credit is reversed later only if the
investigation establishes that you are liable.
</Callout>

If a bank tells you to wait for the investigation to finish before you see any
money, it is describing its own convenience, not the rule. Quote the paragraph in
writing and ask for the response in writing.

The bank then has **90 days** from your report to close the case.

## What to do, in order

Speed beats thoroughness in the first hour. Call first, write second.

## When the bank says no

A refusal is not the end. Two things happen next.

Ask for the rejection **in writing**, with the specific ground. Banks are far
more careful about what they will put in an email than what a call centre will
say on the phone, and an unreasoned written rejection is itself useful evidence.

Then escalate. Thirty days after your complaint, if it is unresolved or rejected,
the RBI Ombudsman is open to you, it is free, and you do not need a lawyer.
[We set out exactly how that works](/disputes/rbi-ombudsman-how-to-file).

## What changes on 1 January 2027

RBI issued Amendment Directions on 24 June 2026 that rework this framework. They
apply to **transactions undertaken on or after 1 January 2027**, so everything
above governs anything that happens to you between now and then.

The headline changes, as announced:

- **A wider concept.** The framework moves from "unauthorised" transactions to **"fraudulent electronic banking transactions"**, which is defined to include transactions executed by a third party using credentials obtained by fraud, and transactions **approved by the customer under coercion or duress**. That second limb matters: some scam transfers you were deceived into approving come inside the framework for the first time.
- **Bank negligence and customer negligence are defined**, rather than left to each bank's policy, and the **burden of establishing customer liability stays with the bank**.
- **A new compensation route for smaller losses.** An individual, including a sole proprietor, losing up to ₹50,000 may receive **85% of the net loss or ₹25,000, whichever is lower**, where the fraud is reported both to the bank and through the National Cyber Crime Reporting Portal or the 1930 helpline **within five calendar days**.
- **Fixed investigation deadlines** — 45 days for domestic complaints, 60 days for cross-border.
- **Shadow reversal within five days** for disputed credit card transactions.
- **Instant SMS alerts** for every electronic banking transaction above ₹500.

<Callout type="warn" title="Report to the cybercrime portal as well, not just the bank">
The new compensation route is conditional on reporting through **both** channels
within five calendar days. That is already good practice today — it is what can
get a beneficiary account frozen — and from 2027 it becomes the condition for a
specific entitlement. Build the habit now.
</Callout>

This page will be rewritten for the new framework before it takes effect. Until
1 January 2027, the 2017 position above is the one your bank is bound by.

<Callout type="alert" title="One year, then the door closes">
A complaint to the Ombudsman must be filed within **one year** of the bank's
reply, or within one year and 30 days of your original complaint if the bank
never replied. Miss it and your only route left is a consumer court.
</Callout>
