This page is about the mechanics and the trap. It is not tax advice, and a
property sale is exactly the situation where you should be paying a chartered
accountant — the fee is trivial against the amounts involved.

## The trap

<Callout type="alert" title="TDS is deducted on the sale price, not on your gain">
When an NRI sells property in India, the buyer must deduct tax at source at the
**non-resident rate**, and the deduction applies to the **entire sale
consideration**, not to the capital gain. On a ₹1 crore sale with a ₹20 lakh
actual gain, the deduction is calculated on the ₹1 crore.
</Callout>

That is the whole problem. A very large sum goes to the income-tax department, and
you get it back only by filing a return and claiming a refund — months later, and
after the buyer has correctly deposited and reported it.

Resident sellers face a much smaller deduction on the same transaction. The
difference catches people who have sold property in India before, as residents,
and expect the same experience.

## The certificate that fixes it

Apply to the income-tax department for a **lower or nil deduction certificate**
under section 197. It is assessed on your actual computed capital gain rather than
the gross price, and the buyer then deducts at the rate the certificate specifies.

Two practical points:

**Start early.** It takes time to process, and it is useless once the sale has
completed and the buyer has already deducted.

**The buyer needs a TAN.** An individual buyer often does not have one and does
not know they need one for this. Raise it at the agreement stage. A buyer who
deducts incorrectly, or deposits late, creates a problem that lands on you when
you try to claim the credit.

## The sequence that works

1. Engage a CA **before** agreeing the sale.
2. Compute the expected capital gain, with the holding period including the previous owner's.
3. Apply for the section 197 certificate.
4. Make sure the buyer has a TAN and understands the deduction obligation.
5. Complete the sale; proceeds go to your **NRO** account.
6. File the Indian return for that year and reconcile the TDS credit.
7. Repatriate, with Form 15CA and the CA's Form 15CB.

## Getting the money out

Proceeds land in the NRO account, and repatriation is capped at **USD 1 million
per financial year** with the 15CA/15CB documentation.
[How that process works](/nri/nro-repatriation-limit-one-million).

If the sale is large, splitting the repatriation across two financial years is a
decision to take **before** the sale, with your CA.

And the proceeds cannot go into an NRE account, however convenient that would be.
[Why the source decides the account](/nri/nre-vs-nro-account-which-one).

## Documents to assemble early

The chain of title for inherited property is where delays come from: the death
certificate, the will or succession certificate, mutation records in your name,
and the previous owner's purchase documents for computing the gain.

Gather these before you list the property. Reconstructing a forty-year-old title
chain from another country, under time pressure from a buyer, is the worst version
of this.
